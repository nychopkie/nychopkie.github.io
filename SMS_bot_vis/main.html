🤖Bots on Social Media

Team: VISVISVIS

Members: WONG Hiu Tung, MA Sum Yi, WANG Ziting, TSE Wai Chung

Part 1 - Overview

This dashboard provides an overview of the distribution of bots and humans across different social media platforms. It highlights the proportion of bot accounts compared to human accounts, as well as the distribution across platforms and language locales.

Key Insights:

  • A significant portion of accounts across all platforms are identified as bots. This might indicate a widespread of artificially created content is found on social media.
  • Language locale distribution shows that English is the most common language among both bot and human accounts, followed by Spanish and Portuguese.
  • Gemini tends to make longer posts. And in general, across all languages and platforms, bots would have longer posts on social media than humans.

Part 2 - Sentiment Analysis of the Posts

Diving deeper into the behaviour of the bots, we analyze the sentiment of the posts made by both bots and humans across different social media platforms.

Key Insights:

  • Bots consistently post more positive content than humans across social media platforms, likely reflecting their promotional purposes. Humans, in contrast, display a sentiment distributions with higher proportions of neutral and negative posts, suggesting more authentic emotional expression.
  • Platform openness shapes sentiment patterns. On public-facing platforms like Twitter and Gab, humans post more positively—aware of their broader audience. However, in private or semi-private spaces like Discord, Telegram, and WhatsApp, humans express more negative sentiment, feeling safer to share sensitive feelings away from public scrutiny. Bots maintain consistently positive messaging across all platforms.
  • Bot sentiment patterns look remarkably similar across languages (~20-30% negative, high neutral), while human sentiment varies significantly by language (e.g., Greek and Scottish Gaelic show much higher negativity). This reveals bots use standardized algorithms that ignore cultural context.
  • While most bot models skew heavily positive, Gemini and opt-iml-max-30b deviate from this pattern. Gemini posts negative content at rates nearly identical to humans, while opt-iml-max-30b also shows elevated negative posts but compensates with a higher proportion of neutral content—suggesting these models may have different training objectives or design philosophies compared to typical promotional bots.

Part 3a - What Topics are Bots chatting about?

Diving deeper into the behaviour of the bots, we analyze the what topics does the bots chat about the most.

Key Insights:

  • The 'Uncertain' category is the most common category, followed by the 'Other' category, which indicates that the bots are not always talking about specific topics and sometimes they just talk meaningless words.
  • Besides the 'Uncertain' and 'Other' categories, the 'Emotions' topic is the most common topic across languages. So the sentiment analysis is very important to analyse the bots' behaviour.
  • The 'Science' topic is the least common topic across platforms, even some languages like Irish and Scottish Gaelic not mentioned the science topic at all.
  • Specifically in English, 'Science' remains the least frequent topic for every platform and agent. On Twitter/X, we observe no Science instances.

Part 3b - What is the most mentioned word from the bots?

Furthermore, we analyze the word choices of different bots and humans.

Key Insights:

  • Bots, especially Gemini, tend to generate longer sentences.
  • Posts created by bots generally have lower information density compared to human posts. Human-generated posts contain a higher proportion of content words — words that carry specific meaning.
  • Bots use fewer nouns than humans do.
